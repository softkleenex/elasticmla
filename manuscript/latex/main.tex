\title{Mean-Safe Is Not Tail-Safe: Token-Wise Latent Capacity in Multi-Head Latent Attention}
\author{ShangJae Lee \\ Kyungpook National University \\ \texttt{dltkdwo31@knu.ac.kr}}
\date{}

\begin{abstract}
Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA) caches one compressed latent per token, at the same width for every token. How much of that width does each token need? We cast token-wise latent width as a rate allocation problem with two exact properties: cache bytes are affine in the summed per-token rates, so allocations with equal rate sums cost exactly the same, and requiring a token to be safe on the worst $k$ of its future losses, a CVaR-type criterion, orders the safe rates monotonically from the average to the worst reuse offset. Measured on three MLA language models (30M-250M parameters) and on the public, production-trained DeepSeek-V2-Lite, this risk-capacity spectrum is wide and stable. Under a 0.1-nat budget the safe rate averages 7-9\% of the latent width when only the mean future loss is constrained, but 72-79\% when the worst future offset is, and the normalized gap between the two is nearly constant (0.646-0.651 across our three models, 0.70 in DeepSeek-V2-Lite). The gap is not caused by rare catastrophic tokens: 93-97\% of mean-safe tokens still exceed the budget at some future offset, and their mean stays small because losses and gains cancel across the horizon. Rotating the latent basis lowers the worst-offset requirement by about 50 points in our small models but by only 11 in DeepSeek-V2-Lite. We then ask whether a learned router can exploit the spectrum, using a variable-width packed cache and pre-registered exact-byte controls. At equal bytes the router beats random, position-based and token-type placement in three of four configurations, but only ties a per-token lookup table: what it learned is explained by token identity, not by context.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Autoregressive transformer [1] inference stores a key and a value for every past token in every attention layer, and this KV cache, growing linearly with context length and batch size, increasingly limits serving capacity. Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA), introduced in DeepSeek-V2 [2] and retained in DeepSeek-V3 [3], shrinks the cache by storing one low-dimensional latent per token from which per-head keys and values are reconstructed. MLA nevertheless gives every token the same latent width. A natural next step, analogous to adaptive computation [26, 27] and to per-token budget allocation in cache eviction [18, 20], is to give each token only the width it needs. Before building such a system, one has to know what "needed" means and how much it varies.

That depends on the criterion. A cached latent is read by every later query, so truncating it changes the loss at many future positions at once. A token can be "safe" in the sense that its average effect over the next $H$ positions is small, or in the stricter sense that no single future position is harmed by more than a budget $\epsilon$. The usual intuition is that the two nearly coincide: most tokens are harmless, and a few rare tokens cause large spikes. If that were true, a router could compress aggressively and protect the rare spikes.

We test this directly and find that it is not true. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}

\item \textbf{A rate-allocation formulation with two exact properties} (Section 3). The persistent bytes of a variable-width packed MLA cache are an affine function of the summed per-token rates, so any two allocations with the same rate sum cost exactly the same bytes; this is what makes byte-exact comparisons between allocation policies possible. And the safe rate under a "worst $k$ of $H$ future losses" criterion, the empirical conditional value-at-risk [33] of a token's future-loss vector, is monotone in $k$ for every token and every realized loss curve, without any smoothness or monotonicity assumption.

\item \textbf{The risk-capacity spectrum} (Section 5). On three MLA models spanning an 8x parameter range and on DeepSeek-V2-Lite, the mean safe rate rises from 7-9\% of the latent width under the mean criterion to 72-79\% under the worst-offset criterion, through every intermediate tail level, and the normalized tail-capacity premium is nearly scale-invariant (0.646-0.651; 0.704 for DeepSeek-V2-Lite).

\item \textbf{A mechanism.} The gap comes from pervasive cancellation, not rare spikes: at the rate that is safe on average, 93-97\% of tokens still exceed the budget at some future offset, and the positive-part mean loss is 2.05-2.31x the signed mean.

\item \textbf{A basis-dependence result with a negative half.} In our models a learned rotation of the latent basis removes about 50 points of the worst-offset requirement, but in DeepSeek-V2-Lite only 11: the tail requirement is real, while the large rotation gain is specific to small models trained on TinyStories.

\item \textbf{A pre-registered case study of learned allocation} (Section 6). A contextual router with a variable-width packed cache beats exact-byte random allocation in three of four frozen configurations and beats position and token-type placement at exactly the router's bytes, but it only ties a per-token lookup table fitted to its own decisions.

\end{itemize}

The spectrum is the paper's main result and does not depend on the router. The router result is narrower, and we report it as it is: a learned allocator can beat naive placements, but we find no evidence that context beyond the current token helps it. We also do not claim serving gains. In our prototype the packed cache stores 60-67\% of the bytes of a full MLA cache, with essentially unchanged peak memory and a 2.4x decode-latency cost (Section 7).

\section{Background and Related Work}

\subsection{Multi-head Latent Attention}

For hidden state $h_t$, MLA computes a compressed KV latent

\[c_t = W_{DKV}h_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_c},\]

and reconstructs content keys and values as $W_{UK}c_t$ and $W_{UV}c_t$. A separate rotary key $k_t^R \in \mathbb{R}^{d_R}$ carries positional information. A fixed-width MLA cache stores $(c_t,k_t^R)$ instead of per-head keys and values; for sequence length $T$, $L$ layers and element size $b$ its leading storage term is $bTL(d_c+d_R)$. MLA continues a line of work that shares or compresses key-value heads, including multi-query [22] and grouped-query attention [23]. MHA2MLA [4], TransMLA [6] and CARE [8] convert pretrained multi-head or grouped-query models to MLA, the last allocating rank across layers with a covariance-aware decomposition; EG-MLA [7] gates latent content with a token-embedding signal; and Cross-Layer Latent Attention combines latent compression with layer sharing and quantization [5]. Analyses of trained MLA models find that latent coordinates separate content from positional information in a way that depends on the basis used to read them [9]. All of these keep one latent width per layer for every token.

\subsection{KV-cache compression}

KV-cache compression methods differ in which axis they compress. Eviction keeps a subset of tokens, selected by accumulated attention (H2O [10], Scissorhands [19]), attention sinks and a recent window (StreamingLLM [11]), or attention patterns observed during prefill (SnapKV [12]); FastGen [20] picks a policy per head, and PyramidKV [13] and Ada-KV [18] allocate different budgets to different layers or heads. Quantization lowers the precision of every cached element (KIVI [14], KVQuant [15]). Low-rank methods project keys and values into a smaller space, uniformly or with layer-wise ranks (Palu [16], LoRC [17], Eigen Attention [21]), and MiniCache [24] merges states across adjacent layers. Dynamic Memory Compression [25] learns per token whether to append or merge into the cache.

Our object is different from each of these. We keep every token and every head, and vary the \textit{width} of each token's latent through a nested prefix, the same nesting idea as Matryoshka representations [30]. This makes per-token rate the only thing that varies, which is what lets us measure how much each token needs and compare allocation policies at byte-identical budgets.

\subsection{Adaptive allocation and its evaluation}

Learning where to spend a discrete budget is a general pattern: adaptive computation time [26], mixture-of-depths [27] and mixture-of-experts routing all learn an importance signal and spend compute nonuniformly. Our router is trained with a straight-through estimator [28] through the hard tiers it uses at deployment. Two evaluation points from this setting carry over. First, an allocation policy should be compared with alternatives at exactly its own budget, or the comparison measures budget rather than placement. Second, the alternatives should include simple causal rules, not only random placement: a router can beat random placement while learning nothing that a lookup table could not. Section 6 applies both.

\section{Token-Wise Latent Rate Allocation}

\subsection{Nested latent prefixes and packed storage}

For each layer $\ell$, calibration produces a permutation $\pi_\ell$ of the $d_c$ latent channels by gradient-times-activation saliency. A token assigned rate $r_t$ stores the nested prefix

\[\tilde c_{\ell,t}=c_{\ell,t}[\pi_\ell(1:r_t)].\]

The packed cache stores a flat values buffer, int32 offsets, int16 ranks, an int16 channel permutation and the uncompressed RoPE key. Layer 0 is always kept at full width (Section 6.1). For a batch-one sequence its exact tensor-payload byte count is

\[M_{\text{packed}}=
b\left[Td_c+(L-1)T\bar r+TLd_R\right]
+4L(T+1)+2LT+2Ld_c,\]

where $\bar r=T^{-1}\sum_t r_t$ and the last three terms count offsets, ranks and permutations; we use $b=4$. This counts the persistent tensors only, not allocator rounding, router weights or the dense temporary latent that the prototype reconstructs during attention (Section 7 measures those).

\subsection{Two propositions}

We do not call $r_t$ an intrinsic rank. MLA's latent can be rotated while compensating the up-projections ($c\mapsto Qc$, $W_U\mapsto W_UQ^{-1}$), and a nested coordinate prefix is not invariant under this change: the full model is, its truncations are not. $r_t$ is therefore an operational rate in a chosen nested codebook $P_{\ell,r_1}\preceq\cdots\preceq P_{\ell,d_c}=I$, and Section 5.4 measures how much the choice of codebook matters.

\textbf{Proposition 1 (exact budget affinity).} For any two per-sequence allocations $\mathbf r,\mathbf s$, $M_{\text{packed}}(\mathbf r)-M_{\text{packed}}(\mathbf s)=b(L-1)\sum_t(r_t-s_t)$. In particular, allocations with the same rate sum have identical cache bytes. \textit{Proof.} Immediate from the byte formula, whose only allocation-dependent term is $b(L-1)T\bar r$.

Proposition 1 is what licenses exact-byte controls: an alternative placement of the same rate sum is a comparison at precisely the same memory, not an approximately matched one.

Let $\Delta_{t,j}(r)=\ell_{t+j}(P_rc_t)-\ell_{t+j}(c_t)$ be the signed change in next-token cross-entropy at future offset $j\in\{0,\ldots,H-1\}$ when only source token $t$ is truncated to rate $r$. For a tail count $k$, let $A_{t,k}(r)$ be the mean of the $k$ largest of the $H$ values $\Delta_{t,j}(r)$. This is the empirical conditional value-at-risk [33] of the future-loss vector at level $\alpha=1-k/H$: $k=H$ gives the signed mean, $k=1$ the maximum. Define the suffix-safe rate

\[r^*_{t,k}(\epsilon)=\min\{r\in\mathcal R: A_{t,k}(r')\le\epsilon\ \text{for every}\ r'\in\mathcal R,\ r'\ge r\},\]

the smallest rate from which every larger rate on the grid $\mathcal R$ is also safe.

\textbf{Proposition 2 (risk-capacity ordering).} If $k_1\ge k_2$, then $r^*_{t,k_1}(\epsilon)\le r^*_{t,k_2}(\epsilon)$ for every token, budget and grid. \textit{Proof.} The mean of the $k$ largest elements of a fixed vector cannot decrease as $k$ shrinks, so $A_{t,k_1}(r)\le A_{t,k_2}(r)$ at every $r$. Every rate that is suffix-safe for $k_2$ is therefore suffix-safe for $k_1$, and the minima are ordered.

The ordering holds for every realized loss vector, including the nonmonotone rank-loss curves we observe (Appendix G), and needs no assumption about the sign or shape of $\Delta_{t,j}$. It is why the result in Section 5 is a whole spectrum of tail levels rather than two endpoints. Under a bounded-Lipschitz assumption on the future logit map, truncation also satisfies $|\Delta_{t,j}(r)|\le\sqrt2K_{t,j}\|e_t(r)\|_2$, where $e_t(r)$ is the discarded latent tail and $K_{t,j}$ a local Lipschitz constant: a monotone safety bound consistent with, but weaker than, the realized ordering.

\subsection{Measuring safe rates}

For each probed source position $p$ and each rate $r$ on the grid, we zero the channels outside the layer's nested prefix at position $p$ in every layer simultaneously, keep every other position at full width, and record $\Delta_{p,j}(r)$ for $j=0,\ldots,H-1$, starting with the prediction made at position $p$ itself, the first one the truncated latent can affect. We use $H=32$ and $\epsilon=0.10$ nat. Calibration data, calibration windows and evaluation windows do not overlap. The rate grid runs from 16 to $d_c$ in steps of 16 up to 64 and steps of 32 thereafter, so every requirement is resolved up to full width. We report, for each tail count $k\in\{32,16,8,4,2,1\}$, the mean of $r^*_{p,k}$ over probes divided by $d_c$, and the tail-capacity premium $\mathrm{TCP}=\mathbb E[r^*_{p,1}-r^*_{p,32}]/d_c$, the average extra width a token needs to be safe at its worst offset rather than on average.

\subsection{The gap comes from cancellation, not rare spikes}

If the gap were caused by a few catastrophic tokens, most tokens that are safe on average would also be safe at every offset. They are not. At the rate that is safe under the mean criterion, 93.1\% (30M), 94.1\% (122M) and 96.1\% (250M) of probes still have at least one future offset whose loss increase exceeds $\epsilon$, and the positive-part mean loss, the mean of $\max(\Delta,0)$ over the horizon, is 2.05x, 2.31x and 2.15x the signed mean (Figure 1(b)). Truncating a token's latent hurts some future predictions and helps others; the signed mean is small because the two cancel, not because the harm is small. A mean-criterion router therefore does not under-provide for a few rare tokens. It under-provides for almost every token, at some offset.

\subsection{The spectrum replicates on DeepSeek-V2-Lite}

On DeepSeek-V2-Lite (last column of Table 2) the spectrum is monotone and close to ours at every tail level, somewhat heavier at the far tail: its premium, 0.704 (95\% CI [0.676, 0.731]), is above the 0.646-0.651 of our three models and the intervals do not overlap, so we claim the same shape and magnitude, not the same value. The cancellation signature also replicates: at the mean-safe rate the positive-part mean loss is 2.10x the signed mean, and 96.6\% of probes still have an offset above $\epsilon$. One qualification: 275 of the 384 coordinate-basis probes reach the mean criterion at the grid's smallest rate (16, 3.1\% of $d_c$), so the 8.22\% mean is an upper bound and the premium a lower bound.

\subsection{How much of the tail requirement is the basis?}

Every rate above is measured in the model's own latent coordinates, ordered by saliency: a permutation, the most restricted nested codebook. We reran the identical probe in rotated orthonormal bases calibrated on the same tokens: principal directions of $\mathbb E[cc^\top]$ ("pca"), eigenvectors of the first-order loss-effect form $\mathrm{sym}(\mathbb E[gc^\top])$ ("grad"), and principal directions in the metric the up-projections apply to the latent ("model metric"). The rotation is folded into the weights, so it leaves the full-width model unchanged and makes a prefix mask a projection onto the leading directions (Appendix D).

This is a caution about what our small models can say on their own, not about the spectrum, which the production model reproduces. Because the deployed packed cache and the router of Section 6 both use the coordinate basis, the coordinate-basis spectrum is also the one that bounds them.

\section{Can a Learned Router Exploit the Spectrum?}

The spectrum says that average-case truncation is cheap and worst-case truncation is expensive, and that the requirement varies widely across tokens. A per-token allocator should be able to exploit that variation. We test whether a learned one does, and against what.

\subsection{Router and training}

Layer 0 runs at full width. A two-layer MLP reads the normalized state entering layer 1, which has already been updated by full-width layer-0 attention over the context, and selects one tier for the token; all downstream layers use that tier. Keeping layer 0 full avoids the circularity of compressing the state that decides the compression. The 30M and 250M-coarse policies use four tiers (6.25\%, 25\%, 62.5\% and 100\% of $d_c$); the 122M and 250M-fine policies use 14 and 18 tiers on the grid of Section 3.3.

Deployment uses the hard choice $y_t=\text{onehot}(\arg\max a_t)$. Training passes gradients through it with a straight-through estimator [28], $\hat y_t = y_t + p_t-\operatorname{stopgrad}(p_t)$ with $p_t=\operatorname{softmax}(a_t/\tau)$, so that the forward pass is exactly the deployed hard rollout, and minimizes

\[\mathcal J(\theta)=\mathcal L_{LM}(\hat y)+\lambda\frac{1}{Td_c}\sum_t\sum_{k}p_{t,k}\mathcal T_k\]

with the base model frozen. Isolated-token capacity labels, the obvious supervised target, are not joint-rollout labels: an early router trained on them collapsed toward the smallest tier when every token was compressed at once, and second-order interaction measurements confirm that isolated labels are misaligned with joint truncation, although only locally (Appendix E). $\lambda$ was chosen on four validation sequences as the cheapest candidate within +0.15 nat of full MLA that also beat an exact-byte static control. Appendix A gives the grids, the chosen $\lambda$ values and the initialization.

\subsection{Against random and shuffled placement at equal bytes}

The primary control spends the router's exact per-sequence rate sum $R=qT+m$ by giving rate $q+1$ to $m$ random positions and $q$ to the rest, averaged over 20 draws: the same bytes as the router (Proposition 1), placed independently of content. A configuration passes when the 95\% interval of the paired per-sequence router-minus-control loss lies below zero (Table 4).

\subsection{Against simple causal rules at equal bytes}

Random and shuffled placement are weak baselines. A cheap causal rule, depending only on the current token or its absolute position, might place the same bytes as well. We fit four such rules as predictors of the router's own tier on the 16 training sequences: absolute position, token identity (a frequency-smoothed per-token lookup table), inverse token frequency, and coarse token type. Each rule receives only the router's per-sequence rate sum and distributes it by its own score, unrestricted to the router's tiers, so every comparison is at exactly the router's bytes. These comparisons were run after the confirmations, on the same already-opened windows, and are exploratory.

\subsection{The quality budget does not transfer reliably}

The 30M and 250M-coarse policies stay within the +0.15-nat selection budget on fresh sequences (+0.1001 and +0.1158 nat); the 122M and 250M-fine policies do not (+0.1823 and +0.1659, against +0.1408 and +0.1483 on validation). Budget calibration and allocation quality are separate axes: 122M misses its budget but keeps its advantage over random placement, while 250M-coarse meets its budget and loses that advantage. A deployed system would need a larger calibration set, a risk margin, or fallback to a higher tier.

\section{Limitations}

\textbf{Scale and data.} Our three models are small and trained on TinyStories, with one checkpoint, one training run and one router seed each; intervals do not include training variability, and model size is confounded with training budget. The one external replication, DeepSeek-V2-Lite, covers the spectrum and the basis ablation on one checkpoint, one text distribution and 384 probes; the router was not trained or evaluated on it.

\textbf{What $r^*$ is.} Rank-loss curves are frequently nonmonotone (Appendix G), so $r^*$ is a conservative suffix rule on a discrete grid, not a smooth or uniquely identified intrinsic rank. It measures isolated single-token truncation; joint truncation interacts locally (Appendix E).

\textbf{Router evaluation.} The 250M-fine confirmation reused windows whose coarse-grid outcomes had been seen, and the heuristic comparisons are exploratory. We have not compared against separately optimized global-budget policies, eviction or quantization baselines, or a router and packed cache operating in a rotated basis. Every compressed policy increases loss over full MLA, and task-level generation quality is not evaluated.

\textbf{Auditability.} For 30M and 122M the per-probe raw deltas behind the spectrum were computed on ephemeral cloud storage; only hash-verified aggregate summaries were recovered, so those two spectra cannot be re-audited per probe. The 250M and DeepSeek-V2-Lite raw records are kept and authenticated by hash.

\section{Conclusion}

Asking how much of an MLA latent each token needs has no single answer: it depends on how much of the token's future one insists on protecting. We showed that this dependence is exact and monotone (Proposition 2), and measured it. Safe on average costs 7-9\% of the latent; safe at the worst of the next 32 positions costs 72-79\%, with a nearly constant normalized premium across an 8x range of model size and in a production-trained model. The premium is not paid for rare tokens but for almost every token, whose small average future effect hides harm at some offset. In small models much of that requirement can be rotated away; in DeepSeek-V2-Lite most of it cannot.

For allocation the implication is a warning. A learned per-token router with a genuinely packed cache does beat random and position- or type-based placement at exactly equal bytes, but it does no better than a per-token lookup table, and a router that sees only the past cannot observe the worst future offset that sets the requirement. Two directions follow: allocation criteria and training signals that target the tail directly, and basis choices that reduce the requirement itself, tested on production-trained MLA models where, by our measurements, the room for the latter is smaller than small models suggest.

\section{Router and selection details}

The 4-tier grid is $\{16,64,160,256\}$ at 30M and $\{32,128,320,512\}$ at 250M. The fine grid runs from 16 in steps of 16 up to 64 and in steps of 32 thereafter, giving 14 tiers at 122M ($d_c=384$) and 18 at 250M ($d_c=512$). Candidate $\lambda$ values were trained on 16 sequences and selected on four validation sequences by the rule of Section 6.1. The selected values are $\lambda=0.4$ (30M), $0.8$ (122M), $0.4$ (250M, 4 tiers) and $0.8$ (250M, 18 tiers); the two 250M policies were trained and frozen independently. The 122M and 250M-fine routers start from a random initialization; the other two start from the isolated-position supervised router. At 122M the 4-tier grid produced no candidate within the validation budget, and the 14-tier grid was adopted before any fresh-window run; this is consistent with the tier-resolution explanation of Section 6.2, but means the 30M-coarse / 122M-fine contrast was not designed in advance. Four older development sequences were observed repeatedly during method development; they are exploratory and enter no confirmatory claim.

\section{Confirmation protocol and provenance}

For 30M and 122M, commit \texttt{a4bcc7f} froze each policy file and its SHA-256 hash, the sampling seed (91,827), 24 fresh sequences, 20 control permutations, the primary endpoint and the success rule, before any fresh-window result existed (\texttt{notes/\allowbreak fresh\_confirmation\_protocol.md}). The 250M-coarse and 250M-fine entries were each committed to the manifest before their runs (\texttt{1ee1528}, \texttt{010e670}).

The two departures from a clean pre-registration are these. First, the 250M-fine entry was added after the 250M-coarse result was known, to test the tier-resolution explanation, and reused the same 24 windows (same seed and exclusion set). Its policy was trained and selected on the original training and validation sequences only, so the reuse cannot have tuned it, but it is a second look at windows whose coarse outcomes had been seen. Second, the original cloud commands preceded the later manifest-enforcement arguments; the frozen protocol commit, complete logs and audits bind those results, and future runs fail unless their manifest inputs match.

Fresh windows come from the held-out region and are separated from all 24 earlier oracle and development windows, and from each other, by at least \texttt{block\_size + 1} tokens. \texttt{experiments/\allowbreak audit\_fresh\_confirmation.py} reconstructs the seeded sampler and recomputes every statistic from the stored per-sequence rows; it checks sampling, byte equality and arithmetic, but does not rerun the model. \texttt{experiments/\allowbreak audit\_joint\_training\_replay.py} compared a separately retrained policy with the frozen one and found a bit-for-bit match; the retrained files were not committed, so repeating that check requires retraining. Exact one-sided sign-flip tests enumerate all sign assignments, which assumes exchangeable signs under the null.

\section{Basis ablation details}

With $W_{DKV}$, $W_{UK}$ and $W_{UV}$ bias-free, substituting $U^\top W_{DKV}$, $W_{UK}U$ and $W_{UV}U$ for an orthonormal $U$ leaves the full-width function unchanged while making a prefix mask a projection onto $\mathrm{span}(U_{:,:k})$; this is verified at full width to at most $3.1\times10^{-5}$ logit deviation. The "grad" basis uses eigenvectors of $\mathrm{sym}(\mathbb E[gc^\top])$, whose quadratic form is the mean first-order loss effect of dropping a direction; its diagonal in the coordinate basis is the signed version of the saliency used by the packed cache, so the deployed ordering is the axis-aligned special case of the same criterion. The "model metric" basis uses principal directions of $A^{1/2}\mathbb E[cc^\top]A^{1/2}$ with $A=W_{UK}^\top W_{UK}+W_{UV}^\top W_{UV}$, the frame that minimizes reconstruction error in the metric the model applies to the latent.

In our three models the worst-offset requirement under principal directions is 23.56\%, 23.25\% and 21.89\% (paired falls 50.5, 49.7 and 49.9 points). Under the coordinate basis at 30M, 410 of 768 probes need at least 224 of 256 channels, against 3 under rotation; at 250M, 77 of 768 need the full latent under coordinates and none under rotation. Ordering by first-order loss effect is 10-12 points worse than ordering by reconstruction at every scale: the worst-offset criterion binds near a quarter of the width, far from where a linearization taken at full width applies. The model-metric frame wins by under a point at 30M and 250M and ties principal directions at 122M, with about half of all probes tied. Under rotated bases more probes have a nonmonotone rank curve (59\% to 82\% at 30M, 78\% to 98\% at 250M), and rotated-basis mean-criterion values sit on the grid's smallest entry and are upper bounds. The coordinate arm reproduces the main-text endpoints and histograms exactly. Table 8 gives all four bases for DeepSeek-V2-Lite.

\section{Pairwise interaction of truncations}

Isolated-token labels would be joint-rollout labels if truncation effects were additive. We measured the second-order mixed difference $L_{\{t_1,t_2\}}-L_{\{t_1\}}-L_{\{t_2\}}+L_{\emptyset}$, which vanishes for any additive distortion, over 1008 position pairs per scale. It is nonzero, so isolated labels are structurally misaligned with joint routing, but the misalignment is local. As a fraction of the individual effects it is 9.09\%, 12.17\% and 13.17\% at 30M, 122M and 250M for adjacent positions averaged over three tested rates (16.79\%, 23.28\% and 22.78\% at rate 16 alone), decays monotonically with separation, and falls below 1.3\% beyond 32 tokens. It grows with truncation severity (5.69\% at rate 16 to 1.07\% at rate 224 at 30M, averaged over separations). We draw no scale trend: the tested rates are fixed absolute values and so different fractions of $d_c$ at each scale, and with 144 pairs per cell from eight sequences and no interval, the data neither establish nor exclude growth with scale (\texttt{notes/\allowbreak pair\_interaction\_prediction5.md}).

\section{Latency engineering}

As first written, the packed decode step was 167.9x (30M) and 201.1x (122M) slower than full MLA. Three unvectorized Python loops were responsible: \texttt{pack\_latents}, \texttt{unpack\_latents} and \texttt{append\_packed\_latents} rebuilt the whole cached history token by token on every decode step, and \texttt{forward\_cached\_packed} built its rank mask with a nested Python loop. Rewriting all three as vectorized operations (\texttt{repeat\_interleave} with one gather; a broadcast comparison for the mask) cut the regression to 3.00x and 2.96x, verified by 500 random-trial cross-checks against the original implementations with zero mismatches. The append still repacked the whole history each step because the flat buffer was ordered (batch, sequence); reordering it (sequence, batch) makes every new token sort after every stored one, so appending becomes a concatenation. Profiling then showed that the dominant cost had been per-step defensive validation, about 200x the cost of the data movement; every check was kept but moved to where its guarantee is established. This gives the 2.42x and 2.37x of Section 7, measured as a paired benchmark against the previous version inside one T4 job (dense control moved by 1\% or less), and verified by bit-identical end-to-end decode logits, ranks and cache bytes. An earlier version of the benchmark reported a 3.5-4.2\% peak-memory reduction; it was a harness artifact (only the router path released its prefill logits before decoding) and disappears when every path does (\texttt{notes/\allowbreak measured\_cache\_memory\_latency.md}). The 30M decode runs to position 320, past its 256-token training context.

\section{Nonmonotone rank-loss curves and reproducibility}

Raw rank-loss curves are frequently nonmonotone: 83.6\%, 89.3\% and 92.1\% of mean-over-horizon curves and 59.4\%, 69.7\% and 78.0\% of max-over-horizon curves at 30M, 122M and 250M have at least one rise (the grids have 10, 14 and 18 points, so these frequencies are not comparable across scales). This is why $r^*$ uses the suffix rule. An earlier run at 250M stopped the rate grid at 384 and jumped to 512, which rounded every requirement in (384, 512] up to full width and overstated the 250M tail rates by up to 4.3 points; all 250M numbers here come from the corrected rerun with the same seeds, checkpoint, windows and channel orders. The 122M-minus-30M differences in the mean and max endpoints are -0.781 points (95\% CI [-1.617, +0.076]) and -1.156 points ([-3.849, +1.611]).
